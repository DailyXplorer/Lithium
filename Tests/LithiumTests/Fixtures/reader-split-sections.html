Brines, batteries and the long road to cheaper lithium

By A. Writer

7 minutes

Paragraph 01: lithium comes from two places, hard rock mines in Australia and salty brines under the high deserts of Chile, Argentina and Bolivia, and the two could hardly work more differently.

Paragraph 02: a brine operation pumps salty water into wide, shallow ponds, lets the sun evaporate it for a year or more, and then refines what is left into lithium carbonate, slowly, cheaply and with a great deal of water.

Why the ponds matter

Paragraph 03: the ponds are why brine lithium has been the cheapest for decades, but they also tie output to the weather, so a wet season can set a whole year of production back by months.

Paragraph 04: direct extraction promises to skip the ponds entirely, pulling lithium out of the brine with sorbents or membranes in hours rather than months, and returning the water underground.
  • Paragraph 05: faster, in hours rather than months.
  • Less water lost to the air.
  • Less land covered by ponds.

Hard rock, fast and costly

Paragraph 06: hard rock mining is quicker to start, digs spodumene out of open pits, and ships the concentrate, mostly to China, where it is roasted, leached and refined at a high cost in energy.

Paragraph 07: prices swing hard, because new mines take years to open while demand from car makers can double in a couple of seasons, and every swing closes some mines and opens others.

Paragraph 08: recycling is the third source, still small, but every battery that comes back holds lithium, nickel and cobalt that never need to be mined again, refined, or shipped across an ocean.

Paragraph 09: in the end, cheaper lithium depends less on any one breakthrough than on patience, permits and water rights, which is why the industry's maps look the way they do.
