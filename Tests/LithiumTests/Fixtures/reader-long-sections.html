A short history of the lightest metal

I. A stone that burned red

Paragraph 01: in 1800 a Brazilian naturalist, visiting a Swedish island, picked up a grey stone that crackled and burned with a crimson flame, and nobody, for seventeen years, could say what was in it.

Paragraph 02: the answer came from a young chemist in Stockholm, who found a new alkali in the stone, lighter than sodium or potassium, and named it after the Greek word for stone, lithos.

II. Grease, glass and mood

Paragraph 03: for a century lithium was a curiosity, then a lubricant: lithium grease kept aircraft and trucks running through the Second World War, in deserts, in the Arctic, and everywhere between.

Paragraph 04: glassmakers and potters found it lowered melting points and toughened ceramics, while doctors, after a long detour through spa waters and quack cures, found that it steadied mood.

III. The bomb and the battery

Paragraph 05: the Cold War made lithium strategic, since one of its isotopes fed the hydrogen bomb, and for years the American government bought nearly all of it, stockpiling far more than it ever used.

Paragraph 06: the rechargeable battery changed everything again: in 1991 a Japanese company sold the first lithium-ion cells, and within twenty years they were in every phone, laptop and camera.1

IV. The rush

Paragraph 07: electric cars turned a niche into a rush, with mines reopening in Australia, ponds spreading across the Andes, and geologists flying over Nevada, Portugal and Zimbabwe in search of more.

Paragraph 08: and so the stone that burned red on a Swedish island now sits, a few grams at a time, in the pockets of billions of people, most of whom have never heard its story, nor need to.

  1. The first commercial cells powered a camcorder.
