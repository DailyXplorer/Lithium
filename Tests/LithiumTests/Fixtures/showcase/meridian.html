The slow train to the coast

Eleven hours, two mountain passes and one dining car. Why the long way round is having a quiet comeback.

Words by Ines Carver · 8 min read

The train leaves at dusk, when the valley has already gone blue. Nobody on the platform is in a hurry. A man folds a newspaper, a child counts the carriages, and the conductor checks his watch against a clock that has been four minutes late for as long as anyone remembers.

By the first tunnel the windows have turned into mirrors. By the second, the dining car smells of coffee and warm bread, and strangers have started lending each other chargers.

A timetable written for people, not for planes

For twenty years the line lost passengers to a forty-minute flight. Then the night service came back, with real beds, a later departure and a ticket that costs less than the taxi to the airport. Last summer it sold out most weekends.

Nobody pretends it is fast. The route climbs two passes at the speed of a bicycle and waits ten minutes at a station with one bench, so the freight train coming the other way can get through. That wait is where most people look up from their phones for the first time.

The steward, who has worked the line since before the closure, says the passengers have changed more than the train has. They used to ask when they would arrive. Now they ask which side the sea will be on.

Morning, on the left

It is on the left. A little after six the track drops out of the last tunnel and runs along the water for an hour, close enough that spray reaches the glass on windy days. The dining car fills up without anyone announcing breakfast.

At the terminus the platform ends a short walk from the harbor. The same train goes back at dusk.
