The town that runs on a salt flat

How a desert town learned to live with lithium.

Paragraph 01: before the lithium companies came, the town lived on llamas, quinoa and a little tourism, and most young people left for the city as soon as they finished school, if not before.

Advertisement

Paragraph 02: now the main street has a bank, two hardware stores and a hotel, and on weekday mornings pickup trucks line up outside the bakery, their drivers in company jackets, waiting for bread.

Paragraph 03: the mayor keeps a chart on her office wall of the water table under the flat, drawn by hand each month from readings the companies share, and she says it is the first thing she looks at.

We sold water we didn't know we had.

Paragraph 04: farmers on the edge of the flat say their wells give less than they used to, while the companies say their pumping draws from deeper brine that never fed the wells at all, and both sides have studies.

Paragraph 05: a university team has spent three years measuring, and its first report, due next spring, may be the closest thing the town gets to a verdict, though few expect it to settle the argument.

Paragraph 06: meanwhile, royalties pay for a new clinic and a school bus, and the council argues every month about how much to save for the years after the brine runs out, which may be twenty or fifty.

Paragraph 07: the young people who left are starting to come back, some as engineers, some as truck drivers, and a few to open cafés for the visitors who now come to see the ponds rather than the flamingos.

Paragraph 08: whether the town is better off is a question people answer with a shrug, a list of what is new, and then, after a pause, a list of what they worry about, starting with the water.
